6. C1 Reading — The Archive That Shouldn’t Exist 🗃️🕯️

By the time Dr. Evelyn Hart arrived at the municipal archive shortly after midnight, the emergency lights had been flickering for nearly an hour and two members of staff had been searching through the basement stacks without success. A sealed file, which had not appeared in the catalogue that morning, had somehow been placed inside a locked cabinet reserved for documents from the 1970s.

Throughout the previous week, the archivists had been preparing the building for a major inspection. They had checked access logs, repaired damaged shelves, and carried out what they believed was a thorough review of every restricted collection. Though nothing unusual had been reported, one junior archivist later admitted that the electronic lock on the basement door had been failing intermittently. He had assumed the problem was technical and, at the time, thought it was too minor to mention.

What made the discovery more disturbing was not the file itself but the handwriting on the envelope. Evelyn recognised it immediately: it belonged to a historian who had died twelve years earlier. Inside was a report describing a property dispute that officially had not begun until three years after his death. Several dates had been altered in pencil, but the original ink was still visible underneath. Someone had clearly been working through the material very carefully, perhaps for months, before returning it to the archive.

At first, Evelyn suspected that a researcher had planted the file as an elaborate hoax. Yet one detail did not fit. The paper had been stored under controlled conditions for so long that the fibres had aged consistently throughout the document. A modern forgery would have been almost impossible to produce without leaving traces. By 2 a.m., the team had been comparing the report with older records for nearly ninety minutes when they found a second reference to the same dispute—this time in a ledger that had not been opened in decades.

Evelyn now faced two possibilities. Either the official timeline was wrong, or someone had been altering archival records long before the current staff were employed. The second explanation seemed less likely, though it would account for the repeated inconsistencies. She had originally thought the night’s problem was a misplaced file. By dawn, she realised they might have uncovered evidence of a much older deception.
